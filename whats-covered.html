What landlord insurance covers

The standard sections of an Australian landlord policy, and what usually still costs you money.

Source. Section headings and typical excesses below are the common wording across Australian landlord Product Disclosure Statements (GIO, Vero, AAMI, Bank of Queensland, Australia Post). Always read your own PDS — wording and limits vary by insurer.

Standard cover sections

SectionWhat it typically covers
Building damageStorm, cyclone, fire, flood, escape of liquid, and damage caused by your tenants or their guests.
Landlord contentsCarpet, appliances, whitegoods and furniture supplied by you — not items the tenant brought.
Loss of rentRent lost when a tenant fails to pay or vacates without notice, where the policy covers it. Usually carries a waiting period.
Tenant defaultCover for damage caused by a tenant who does not pay or leaves early, depending on the policy wording.
Legal expensesUp to a stated limit for legal costs recovering rent or damage from a tenant.
Escape of liquidBurst pipes and appliance leaks.
Temporary accommodationCost of temporary accommodation while an uninhabitable home is repaired, where the policy allows it.

Excesses you will meet

Most Australian landlord policies carry a base excess of around $1,000 — that is the figure Canstar used for all the averages on this site.

  • Unoccupied property excess — typically $1,000 on top of the base excess if you claim while the property sits empty. This is the single most common trap for landlords whose tenant walks out mid-lease.
  • Earthquake and tsunami excess — commonly an additional $200 to $300.
  • Renovation or structural alteration excess — applies if the property is being modified or has had unapproved work done.
  • Tenant-caused damage — you generally pay the excess, then may recover from the tenant. Insurers are not insurers of the tenant.

What is usually NOT covered

  • Structural defects, rust, damp, or pre-existing damage
  • Deliberate damage, or damage from an unauthorised occupant
  • Unapproved renovations and improvements
  • Loss of rent where the tenant is not in arrears, or where notice periods were not met
  • Contents belonging to the tenant rather than the landlord
  • Underinsurance — declaring a sum insured below realistic replacement cost leaves you short in a total-loss claim

Loss of rent: read the waiting period

Loss of rent cover is usually the section most landlords misunderstand. Most policies impose a waiting period (commonly 14 days) before rent losses become payable, and some exclude the period after the tenant leaves. The rent you are claiming must have been charged at market rate and properly documented with a signed lease.
